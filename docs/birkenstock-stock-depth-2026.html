  • It sold at least 10 pairs this year — below that the weekly pace means nothing — it was at least 20% below RRP, and one of these two applies:

Case study — Arizona Leo Black, and what a 12-pair probe taught us

1030498-ARIZONA · 12 units · £90 RRP

The last row of the flagged table is worth pulling apart, because it is the only style this year that was deliberately traded — bought as a 12-pair sample with no reorder, listed high, cut hard, then raised again. It is the closest thing we have to a controlled experiment on price, and it is the one style where we also know what the ads cost.

The window of action was three weeks, not three months. The stock landed 8 May and sat unsold for 24 days. First sale 1 June, and 11 of the 12 pairs went in the 23 days to 23 June — the top of the June peak. The twelfth took another three weeks and sold on 15 July. Everything that mattered happened inside one month.

Did the £87 test actually prove anything?

  • Zero sales at £87 proves almost nothing on its own. Over its life the style converted at 3.9% (12 sales from 309 clicks). At that rate, 17 clicks predicts 0.7 sales — so zero is the expected result, not evidence of a bad price. The £87 phase never gathered enough traffic to be a test.
  • The click-through rate is the real signal, and it is decisive. At £87 the listing was shown 1,380 times and clicked 17 times — 1.2%. At £72 it clicked at 3.2% on eight times the impressions. In Shopping the price sits inside the ad, so shoppers rejected it before the click. £87 was genuinely priced out of the market.
  • But the return trip was far too timid. The cut was £20 in a single day. The rise back was £5, and then it stopped. £66.99 was obviously too cheap — five pairs in 48 hours — and that was correctly spotted within two days. Yet £71.99 then held for six weeks and emptied the shelf in peak season. The whole £75–£82 band was never tested.
On product margin +£139.85

Avg sold £69.91, 22.3% off RRP, £11.65 a pair — the best profit per unit on the whole flagged list, on a £37.50 cost.

After Google ads −£13.32

Google spent £153.17 driving those 309 clicks. Charge all of it to this style and the 12-pair experiment lost money, despite looking like the best-margin style on the page.

The number that decides it — ad cost per sale

  • Clicks cost £0.50 each here (brand average is £0.41 — this style was slightly expensive traffic). At a 3.9% conversion rate that is £12.72 of advertising per pair sold.
  • So £12.72 a pair is the floor. At £71.99 the margin was £13.05 — dead break-even. At the £66.99 probe price, £9.70, it was losing money on every sale. The style was never priced above the cost of getting the customer.
  • There was no wasted spend at the ends: only £10.95 went on the dead £87 phase, and nothing at all was spent after the last pair sold. The overspend was not sloppiness — it was the price being set below the ad break-even.
  • Raising the price does not fix this for free: a higher price buys a lower click-through and probably a lower conversion rate, which pushes ad cost per sale back up. That is exactly why the untested £75–£82 band matters. Both bands we did test are now proven to fail — £87 on velocity, £72 on margin. The middle is the only place left where this style can work.

Is it a loser, then? No — but it is marginal, and only at the right price. The demand is real: 9,960 impressions and a healthy 3.2% click-through, twelve pairs cleared inside six weeks with sizes 36–42 all selling. That is not a dud. The problem is the shape of the economics — £37.50 cost against a £90 RRP that the market clears nearer £70 — which leaves too little between margin and ad cost. And the −£13.32 is the harshest possible reading: it charges 100% of the ad spend to the style, when some of those twelve pairs would have sold from search, the shop, or the brand's own pull without paying for the click.

Worth another 12 pairs next year? Only on three conditions: land it in April, not May, so the whole June peak is available; open at £78–£79, not £87; and hold that price for a fortnight before touching it. If it does not bite by mid-June, cut once and clear. What is not worth repeating is buying it, listing it at RRP, and discovering in the last week of May that nobody wants it at that price — that alone burned a third of the selling window.

The lesson — cut hard, but the ratchet has to work both ways

  • Cutting aggressively was right. With 12 pairs and no reorder, sitting at a price nobody clicks is the expensive mistake — 24 days of a 45-day selling window went to a price that was never going to sell. A hard cut is the fastest way to find the bite, and it costs little on a small quantity.
  • Treat the cut as a probe, not a price. £66.99 was a question — "is anyone there?" — and it was answered in 48 hours. It should never have been left as the answer.
  • Come back up as hard as you went down, and keep going until the pace drops. We cut £20 and returned £5. The rule should be: after a bite, raise in real steps — £5, then £5 again — and only stop when a week goes by without a sale. Backing off £5 and settling is how a probe turns into a permanent discount.
  • Know the ad break-even before setting the floor. £12.72 a pair is Leo Black's own number, not a brand rule — it is high because this style converted at only 3.9%. Every style has its own floor, and the next section works it out for all 22. But the principle holds: a price that leaves less margin than it costs to buy the click is not a price, it is a subsidy. Both £66.99 and £71.99 failed that test on day one.

Three patterns, and only one of them is about price

  • Narrow fits are where the money goes. The ten Narrow styles sold 305 pairs, made £1,471, and spent £1,860 on ads — −£389. The twelve Regular styles sold 336 pairs and cleared +£749. Narrow converts at 6.1% against Regular's 8.2%, so every Narrow sale costs £6.10 to buy against £5.22. The clearest case is one shoe in two widths: Patent Black Regular made £146; Patent Black Narrow lost £155. Same product, same ad account, opposite outcome.
  • Arizona EVA cannot carry advertising at all. Nine EVA styles, 288 pairs, £904 of margin against £1,239 of ads — −£335. EVA actually converts well (8.0%) and its clicks are the cheapest in the set, so the traffic is not the problem. A £50 RRP discounted to £36 simply does not leave £4.30 of room. Faded Lime Green is the extreme: 12% conversion, 25p clicks — genuinely popular — and it still loses £17, because £1.07 a pair cannot cover £2.09 of advertising.
  • Bend Low Black is the model, and it is not flagged for the reason it looks flagged. 46 pairs, £11.96 a pair, 10.2% conversion, £4.06 to buy a customer, +£363 — the best on the page. It is on this list for being 30% off RRP, but the trade works. Its Narrow twin is far weaker (3.5% conversion, £11.46 a sale, +£56) on more than twice the ad spend. And 81 pairs of the Black are still on the shelf — as autumn stock, that is the one to push now.

What to do with this

  • Set the floor per style, not per brand. Ad cost per sale ranges from £2.09 to £12.76 across these 22. The rule is simply margin per pair must clear that style's own ad cost per sale, with room to spare — and a style sitting under it is being subsidised, not sold.
  • Raise the twelve loss-makers first. They hold 369 pairs of the 641. Most need only £2–£5 to cross over, which is well inside the 25–35% discounts they are already carrying.
  • Treat Narrow as a different product. It converts worse and should not be discounted as deeply as the Regular of the same shoe. Today the discounts are broadly matched across widths, which is why the Narrow half of the range pays for the Regular half.
  • For Arizona EVA, price is not the whole answer. Nine colours cannot each carry their own ad spend at £50 RRP. Either they come off Shopping and sell organically, or they sell nearer £42–£45 — but funding £1,239 of clicks to make £904 is the one thing not worth repeating.

How harsh is this reading? It charges every penny of a style's Google spend to that style, and credits it with no sale it would have made anyway — no organic search, no direct visit, no returning customer. The true position is better than the table shows. But the ranking is what matters, and that holds regardless: the styles at the top of this table are being sold at a loss to buy customers we then discount to.

Overall — too low and too quick, or stuck with it?

Both — but on different shoes. Too cheap on the fast sellers, and too deep on everything else. The second one is much the bigger number.

What counts as a sale. Every sale from 1 January to 7 September 2026, all channels, for products branded Birkenstock. A "style" is the whole product (the groupid), not the individual sizes.

What counts as stock. Sizes left is the number of different sizes with real, sellable stock on the shelf right now — free stock, not deleted, quantity above zero. The old stockvariants figure is not used because it is out of date.

What the money columns mean. Avg sold is the average price a pair actually went out at, after discounts — not the list price. Profit per unit is the average net profit on a pair. Pace is pairs per week between the style's first and last sale this year.

Where the ad numbers come from. The Leo Black case study uses the imported Google product-level daily figures for that style (impressions, clicks, cost, conversions), matched to the price in force on each day from the price change log. Ad cost per sale is the style's own cost per click divided by its own conversion rate over the whole period; it charges every penny of the style's ad spend to the style, so it is a deliberately harsh reading.

A correction to the unit counts. The style tables above count sales rows, and a return is stored as its own row with a quantity of minus one. They therefore count returns as sales: Arizona EVA White Narrow shows 78 units but sold 61 net of returns, and its true margin is £2.77 a pair, not £2.17. The after-ads table uses the corrected net figures throughout, which is why its units are lower. Direction of travel is unchanged everywhere — net margins are slightly better per pair and the volumes slightly smaller.
